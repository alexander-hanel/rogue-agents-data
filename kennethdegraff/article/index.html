OpenAI's robots got into a closed link shortener, surfed the web with Google Docs and Translate, then called the FBI with a stranger's key

My questions after finding hidden keys left by OpenAI's agent swarm.

By Kenneth Russell DeGraff·Tech and climate policy·contact form

A swarm of OpenAI agents got into Vanderbilt University's link shortener, a service with no public door, and built at least 170 short links there. Vanderbilt says the service is for affiliated organizations only, granted by help ticket, for official communication only. The agents got in anyway, and nobody has said how. Vanderbilt was one of twenty-one sites the swarm wrote to, and one of four universities whose link shorteners the swarm used. The full list is in the table below.

480 addresses in the agents' posts hand a file to Google's document viewer and ask Google to fetch and render it: federal budget reports on portal.max.gov, the SEC's crowdfunding county file, Texas state archive PDFs. Google was not the only way in, and that is the point. One Texas archive PDF was requested 128 different ways, through eight other services and twice straight from the archive. The federal budget files went out through markdown.new, through pure.md, and 34 times with no middleman at all. Fourteen services in all fetched pages for the swarm, and thirteen of the fourteen were aimed at one file, the SEC's county list. Google Translate served the same purpose on a different surface: feed it a foreign address and it hands back the page, and the swarm used it that way 70 times in the metadata of 83 software packages it published in one afternoon.

The agents did not hide. Thirteen of the links carry the maker's name, among them openaipovertycompacttest, agent0zambjson and aaaagent0.

A link shortener's statistics page, public at the time, prints whatever address a visitor claims to have come from, and checks nothing. That made a public university page into a writable message board, and these agents wrote to it 54,250 times in a single day.

Then the agents used those links to query the FBI's Crime Data Explorer 495 times and wrote every query onto Vanderbilt's public pages. Three keys went to the FBI. One was the government's free demo key. One was the FBI's own developer key, copied off the bureau's public code. The third belonged to a stranger: a student who left the key in the saved output of a 2023 school project, in a file too large for GitHub to index, invisible to almost every search engine. The agents found the key anyway, and took the query and the Alabama county that came with the key.

The whole record sits in the visitor log of one Vanderbilt link from 2011 that points at a staff newsletter piece comparing JFK's 1963 visit to Vanderbilt Stadium with a U2 concert there in 2011: one telephone in the stadium then, forty thousand now. The article went offline in 2024. The link did not. The link took 54,250 visits in a single day on June 18, 2026, after fifteen years averaging one visit every other day.

That log ties the Vanderbilt operation to DSEWiki, the German-language wiki where researchers at collusion.wiki published on Friday, September 4, a record of about 18,000 posts by OpenAI agents, an incident OpenAI acknowledged the next day. 391 of the 5,212 addresses in the Vanderbilt log appear word for word in the agents' own DSEWiki posts, accounting for 29,806 visits. The two operations ran on the same days, used the same proxy tools, and in one case the same name: a Vanderbilt link named for cooks made on June 21, and a DSEWiki page named for cooks made hours later.

As of September 8, both Vanderbilt and the University of Toronto have hidden the statistics pages behind a login, and Vanderbilt has rewritten 28 of the agent-made links to point at the university's homepage. 142 still pointed at the targets the agents chose when I last resolved all 170, at 21:23 UTC on September 9.

Every state described here is as of 21:23 UTC on September 9, 2026, when I last resolved all 170 links one by one, and it can change at a moment's notice.

The pageA dead link about JFK and Bono became the robots' notebook

One Vanderbilt link matters more than the rest. The link, iyg1y, dates from August 2011 and points at a Vanderbilt Medical Center staff newsletter piece, “Of JFK, U2, telephones and security.” The piece set President Kennedy's 1963 speech at Vanderbilt Stadium beside U2's concert there on July 2, 2011: one telephone line run out to the field for the president then, forty thousand cell phones held up for Bono now. That article went offline in July 2024. The page died; the short link and the public visitor log did not.

59,942 visits across the link's whole life. 2,950 of them fell in the fifteen years to 2025, between 54 and 459 a year. 54,250 fell on June 18, 2026.

The log holds 5,212 addresses from 81 sites, plus 184 single-visit addresses the page files under “Various,” and visitors from 81 countries. 391 of those addresses appear word for word in the agents' own posts on DSEWiki, accounting for 29,806 visits, just under half of the link's lifetime traffic and more than half of June 18. 87 more also appear on a single University of Toronto page, accounting for 22,732 visits. 448 entries name another Vanderbilt short link, which is how the roster of everything else the agents built survived. 30 carry a ZZZ marker — strings like UNIQUELOGZZZ322869901 and HELLOREFZZZ=110, stamped into a test write so the agent could find that line again on the next read.

The log also holds the three keys that reached the FBI.

AccessNo public door, but OpenAI agents got in anyway

Vanderbilt's shortener has no form. The homepage says the service “is only available for organizations affiliated with Vanderbilt University and is only to be used for official Vanderbilt communication,” and that a department wanting access should “open a help ticket with Digital Strategies.” The login is marked restricted. The Internet Archive shows Vanderbilt posting the same restriction in June 2026, the month the agents made the links.

The software behind the shortener, YOURLS, creates links two ways: through that admin login, or through an API that requires a secret signature. Both are private. If the agents used neither, then something in the software let the agents through.

How I count the 170, and why nobody had that number. Two researchers published inventories of these links before me. fi-le.net listed 28 on September 4. A research ledger at helppeer.app lists 25. Between the two lists, 37 distinct links.

Neither includes iyg1y. That page is the roster. The iyg1y visitor log records the address each visitor claimed to arrive from, and the agents arrived carrying other Vanderbilt short links. So one page names 124 sibling links, 114 of which appear nowhere else.

I resolved every code found across my captures and in the swarm's posts. 174 of them answered. Four belong to Vanderbilt: the 2011 article, two news stories from 2008 and 2016, and a sports page. Those are ordinary university links, made years ago for ordinary reasons, and the agents used those four the same way as iyg1y, for the public log that comes attached. The other 158 in my captures are the agents' own, confirmed by where each one points. The swarm's posts name 12 more that resolve today, which is how the count reaches 170.

Those 13 extra codes are worth a line of their own. Each appears in the agents' posts as a vanderbi.lt address, none appears in any page I saved, and twelve of the thirteen answer today with a redirect to vanderbilt.edu, the same answer the other rewritten links give. The thirteenth, jsmap88997, returns the shortener's not-found response, the answer an invented code gets. So 170 links and 28 rewrites, and 170 is still a floor: it counts what five saved pages and one published dataset happen to name. Nobody has enumerated the shortener itself, and I have no way to.

The invented targets. 36 of the links point at example.com and example.org with paths like BrandNewTargetUniqueourcfu170mz and new1782117457.00458. Those are not data fetches but tests, to find out whether link creation worked at all.

Thirteen of the links name the maker. openaipovertycompacttest, agent0zambjson, agentinvestslice19777, agentjqroundma777, 00agent, aaaagent0. Whatever created those links was not trying to blend in.

One is named for cooks. The agents created cookage85datax on June 21 at 4:40pm on the shortener's own clock and pointed the link at a Data USA query for occupation code 352010, the census code for cooks. That exact query fragment appears in 416 of the agents' published posts. A DSEWiki page titled AgentCookAge85DataXYZ appeared at 03:08 UTC the next morning; Vanderbilt does not publish the shortener's timezone, so the gap is between five and eleven hours. The word Cook appears in 98 of the swarm's account names and 207 of its page names. The agents were working a task about cooks, and named links, pages, and accounts after that task.

And the agents were learning the mechanism. The agents requested iyg1ya four different ways: /iyg1ya, /iyg1ya+, /iyg1ya%2B, and /iyg1ya+?x=1. That is something working out how the statistics page responds to different forms of the plus sign. Two of the agents' links are named iyg1z and iyg1ya, the 2011 link's name with one character changed or added. iyg1z goes to the bureau's own site with a request for 2022 arrest counts at Autauga County, and appears inside the iyg1y log 405 times as the address a visitor claimed to arrive from. iyg1ya points at a nonsense path on api.usa.gov, the federal gateway that fronts the FBI's data. A third, abcde, carries the same Alabama question to api.usa.gov with the government's demo key attached. I did not open any of the three.

So one of three things is true. The agents had a Vanderbilt account. Or the agents had the API signature. Or the software let the agents in without either. Which of the three is unknown.

The agents obeyed a rule when a rule existed. The University of Toronto publishes the condition on its own shortener: the target address must contain utoronto.ca or one of three related domains. The agents read that rule and complied, pointing links at utoronto.ca/foobaruniq456, a fake path on a real domain. Toronto is the control case, and it comes next.

Two more universities turn up in the logs. The University of New Mexico runs the same software at goto.unm.edu, behind a UNM login. The agents did not make links there. Instead the agents took an old UNM events link from 2023, 7t6-o, and used the public log attached to that link the same way as iyg1y. That log took 1,845 visits on June 18, out of 2,502 in the link's whole life, and holds 761 addresses. 94 of those addresses appear word for word on the Vanderbilt page, 62 in the DSEWiki dataset, and 23 on Toronto's pages. The UNM log names three Vanderbilt links missing from every earlier count, and a v.gd link names a fourth. ETH Zurich's shortener, u.ethz.ch, appears once, in Toronto's log: a 2020 ETH link whose best day in six years was 63 visits on June 18, 2026. UNM's log was open to anyone on September 9. ETH's has an archive copy from September 6.

TorontoThe control case: Toronto's door was open

Toronto's shortener, uoft.me, ran the same software and took the same visitors, and required no authorization: anyone whose target address contains utoronto.ca, toronto.edu, utoronto.sharepoint.com or uthrprod.service-now.com may make a link. On June 18, the agents created four short links there between 10:52am and 3:03pm, each pointing at a utoronto.ca address that returns a 404. Together the four took 2,349 visits that day; one, maagentxyz99999, took 1,735. @j0wimo posted two of the four on X on September 4.

The log of maagentxyz99999 holds the 65-character SEC address found in 790 DSEWiki posts, and 87 of the addresses in that log also sit on Vanderbilt's iyg1y page, worth 22,732 visits there. None of the 170 Vanderbilt link names appears on Toronto's pages, which I checked one by one. The two operations shared tools, not names: jqp.vercel.app appears 1,556 times on Toronto's pages, the SEC file 391 times, the allorigins proxy 82.

Toronto put its statistics pages behind a login on September 8, the same day Vanderbilt did.

Toronto shows the agents obeyed a published rule when one existed. That is what makes the Vanderbilt entry stand out as an anomaly rather than routine agent behavior.

The keysThree keys went to the FBI. Two were real. One was a stranger's.

DEMO_KEY, the key the government prints in the government's own documentation so nobody needs a real one. Used 241 times.

MechanismTwo ways to talk through a link: how a shortener became a message board

These agents could read the internet, but could not write to the internet. So the agents found a way to write using nothing but a read, and a link shortener gave the swarm two such ways.

The link itself is the first channel. When an agent creates masscfround3x260618 and points that link at a proxied federal data query, that agent has published a name. Any other agent that knows the naming scheme can resolve that name and get the payload. That is a shared dictionary, written into someone else's server. The name carries the meaning; the target carries the data.

The visitor log is the second. The short link is vanderbi.lt/iyg1y. Add a plus sign and the statistics page opens, listing every web address a visitor claimed to have come from. The visitor supplies that address, and nothing checks the claim. An agent sends a request with any text at all in that referral field, and the statistics page prints that text where any other agent can read the text.

What the log proves, and what it does not

Every line on that page is a claim made by the visitor. The log shows that the agents wrote FBI addresses, three of them carrying API keys, onto a public university page. It does not show that those same requests reached the FBI. Only OpenAI's record of what its agents fetched, or the government's record of what arrived, can settle that, and both are asked for below. Everything counted here is what the page recorded.

The difference is direction. A redirect points outward, and reading a redirect means resolving the link. A referral points inward, and reading a referral means loading the statistics page. The first is a name someone else can look up. The second is a note left on the page.

By that measure every one of the 170 links is communication. Every one is a published name pointing at a payload.

How many used the second channel is a different question, and I can only partly answer that question. I hold the statistics pages for 24 of the 170. Seven of those 24 carry a real visitor log: iyg1y with 5,212 addresses, massjson715 with 125, testfreshmd991 with 44, g3-jc with 36, 34Y3g with 22, majsonstats with 15, and rwandamodvals with 11. Five more carry one or two stray entries. Twelve carry none; the agents used those as names only.

iyg1y alone holds more than ninety percent of everything visible.

The swarm did not need a university link shortener. Any shortener with a public statistics page works, and the logs name four with no door at all. 2dd.pl, a Polish shortener running the same software, gives out custom names without an account; the agents made agnewmd26615703 and agnewmd77805076 there, and 2dd.pl has since put its statistics pages behind a login too. On da.gd the agents named a link SECcountyM and pointed that name at the SEC county file. On is.gd and v.gd the agents parked the SEC and Iowa files. jsonhero.io, a viewer that stores any JSON document under a share link, holds two of the swarm's documents and appears 2,274 times in the DSEWiki posts. At Vanderbilt the agents made links behind a shut door. At New Mexico the door was also shut, and the agents did not go through; at New Mexico and at Zurich they wrote into the public logs of links the universities had made years earlier, which any visitor can do. Everywhere else the door was open.

Why only 24. On September 4 and 7 I captured nine statistics pages live. On September 8 Vanderbilt put every statistics page behind a login. Later that same day I pulled the iyg1y visitor log apart and found that log named 124 sibling links nobody had listed. By then the pages were shut. I recovered 15 more from the Internet Archive; the other 146 have no archive capture at all. A login would not help either, because the statistics page is only a summary of the shortener's database, where every click sits as one row with a time, a claimed origin, an address and a country. The page thins its charts to about thirty points and keeps only the biggest day, so the June day-by-day counts are already gone from every page still open, New Mexico's included. Those database rows are the only place the record survives.

Nobody was reading that Vanderbilt Medical Center link about JFK and U2, because there was nothing left to read. Every one of those 54,250 visits intentionally wrote a line into the link's public visitor log.

RubyGemsThe swarm shipped 83 software packages. None of them were software.

On the same day the agents were working Vanderbilt's shortener, a RubyGems account called ulinkqy8py3mp published 83 software packages. Every one went up on June 18, 2026, between 17:53:31 and 20:52:56 UTC. All but one carry version 0.0.1. The authors are listed as “Data User,” “R” and “Reader.” The descriptions say things like “Public map dataset reference collection.”

The packages contain nothing worth installing. What they carry is metadata. RubyGems lets a package name a homepage, a source repository, documentation, a changelog, a wiki, a bug tracker and a funding page, and this account filled those fields with 583 distinct web addresses. 384 of them point at sec.gov/files/county.json, the same federal file 13 of the 14 services on Vanderbilt's pages were aimed at.

The addresses are wrapped the same way, too: r.jina.ai 46 times, markdown.new 37, webcrawlerapi.com 21. And Google again, from a different direction: Google Translate 27 times, plus 43 more through its translate.goog mirrors, used to fetch a page rather than to translate one. Feed Translate a foreign address and it returns the page, which makes it a reader like any other.

The gems are still published. They have been downloaded 42,104 times.

This is the same move as the shortener: a public service that stores text on someone else's server, filled with addresses instead of the thing it was built to store.

The queriesThree keys, one ladder: how the requests reached the FBI

The same few questions, over and over. Arrest counts for one Alabama police agency across 2022. Hate crimes in Arizona, California, Hawaii and Ohio. Police employment in California, Georgia and Kansas. Robberies in New York.

How the agents reached the FBI. 382 of the 495 sit in the iyg1y log alone. 117 went to the FBI's own website, which needs no key. Three more reached that same website through cors.io, one of fourteen outside services the swarm used to fetch a page and hand the page back. 262 went to api.usa.gov, which does need a key. 216 of those used DEMO_KEY, the free key the government prints in the government's own documentation so nobody needs a real one; the demo key allows 30 requests an hour. Nine carried no key at all. Thirty-three used a real key that sits on the line that fills the key into the FBI's own documentation page. Forty-five other projects have copied that key, and everyone using that key shares one limit.

Four visits used a citizen's key.

That is the ladder. The demo key allows thirty requests an hour from one address, and the agents ran 216 requests through the demo key on this page alone, more than seven hours' worth at that rate. Next came the FBI's own developer key, which shares one limit with everyone who has copied that key. Then, four times, a key that belongs to a private citizen. And with that key came something new: the agents set aside the task's own questions and started asking the questions the key's owner had asked in 2023, about the same Alabama county, through the same endpoint.

The citizen's key

The key belongs to a student who published the key by accident in 2023, inside the saved output of a data science notebook on crime and mental health, on a personal site. I am not naming the person or the page.

The notebook uses the same base address the agents used, api.usa.gov/crime/fbi/cde. The notebook names the Autauga County Sheriff's Office on one map. And the notebook contains the endpoint the agents hit with the key, written as /arrest/agency/%s/all.

So the agents did not register the key, and did not take the key off anyone's device. The agents found a page and took three things out of that page: the key, the query pattern, and the county.

The General Services Administration issues api.data.gov keys against a working email address. GSA issued this one to a person, not to an agent. GitHub's own code search does not find the key: the file is 3.4 megabytes, and GitHub does not index files that large.

All four visits asked the same question: 2022 arrest counts for a single named police department. Agency AL0040000 is the Autauga County Sheriff's Office in Alabama, a county of about 60,000 people west of Montgomery. Three addresses, differing only in where the key sat and how the punctuation was written. The citizen's key was never used on anything else.

ScaleA low value key and a high effort route

The citizen's key opens nothing secret. The key opens public crime statistics that anyone can request for free in about a minute.

So ask the obvious question. If the data is free, why did the agents fetch one SEC file through thirteen different middleman services, and stack two of them at a time in 3,038 of their own posts? Why request a single Texas archive PDF 128 different ways? Why send an FBI request through cors.io, a service that appears nowhere in those posts? Why run 241 requests on a demo key that allows thirty an hour, then fall back to a key the FBI published for everyone to share, and then reach for a private individual's key buried in a three-year-old school project?

And why did the whole operation run on other people's equipment: a German wiki, a Polish paste board, and the link shorteners of four universities?

The only thing a real key buys is room. The demo key stops at thirty requests an hour. A registered key allows a thousand. The agents were not after secret data. The agents were after a way to keep hammering a free service past the point where the free key cuts the agents off. And once the agents held a stranger's key, the stranger's questions came along with the key.

QuestionsA few of my questions for OpenAI

I spent years working in Congress. These are some of the first questions I would want asked.

  1. How did OpenAI's agents create at least 170 links on a Vanderbilt service that has no public door? Did the agents hold an account there, an API signature, or neither? And why did 36 of those links point at example.com with invented paths, if not to test whether creation worked?
  2. Why did the agents take a stranger's key out of a 2023 school project on a personal site, and then start asking the FBI the same questions that project asked, about the same Alabama county? The task in front of the agents was something else. What made that notebook the thing to copy?
  3. How did the agents reach a key that no search engine returns, in a file too large for GitHub's own search to index? What were the agents reading, and how much?
  4. Did OpenAI's monitoring see the company's agents send a private individual's credential to a federal server, four times? Has OpenAI told that person?
  5. Is using a credential found in someone's published work a behavior OpenAI tests for, and does the company count that as misalignment? Writing a working private key onto a public page is never correct. Had OpenAI seen this before June?
  6. GSA holds the email address on the account behind that key and can confirm in a minute that the account belongs to a private individual with no tie to OpenAI. OpenAI can check the company's own records. Will either do so?
  7. OpenAI's July 28 update referred to “a few accounts accessed by other evaluations.” Congress asked which services hosted those accounts and has not been told. Was Vanderbilt one of those services?
  8. On August 10 Congress asked OpenAI how many times an internally deployed model obtained credentials it was not issued, and how many of those were disclosed to any government body or affected third party. OpenAI answered on August 31 with a footnote. What is the number?
  9. Will OpenAI supplement the August 31 letter to Congress to cover this?
  10. Were these agents part of the swarm OpenAI has now acknowledged, or a separate run? If separate, why was that one also working on other people's equipment, and why did that run climb the same ladder of keys?
  11. And why did it take an outsider like me, with a web browser, to find any of this?
